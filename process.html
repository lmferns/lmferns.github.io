Design Process

From messy data to a build that works.

Before I build anything, I find out what's actually going wrong, get the real stories out of SMEs, and map the logic so the build goes smoothly.

  • Logic mappingI turn SME input and technical guides into wireframes, mapping the if/then logic before anyone opens Storyline.
  • Branching scenariosI design decision trees based on real troubleshooting and clinical workflows, with feedback tied to what the learner chose.

Requirements & Analysis: Closing a Performance Gap

  • Needs analysis
  • Data synthesis
  • Certification design
35%ticket escalation rate, traced to specific skill gaps

Background

Medical Group Inc* makes medical devices and trains the remote and field service engineers who install and maintain them in hospitals. Audits showed a rise in support tickets caused by improper device handling, slow troubleshooting and communication problems that affected patient care.

The challenge

Engineers had a 35% ticket escalation rate and weren't engaging with the legacy LMS content. Support tickets, focus groups and on-site observations showed that half of learners found the content too technical, which led to missed safety steps and troubleshooting errors.

What I did

  • Pulled the data into a performance gaps overview to rank the highest-risk problems, like escalations and safety lapses.
  • Matched each gap to a fix. For example, the lack of device-specific troubleshooting skills led to interactive, scenario-based modules.
  • Proposed a certification program tied to performance exams and satisfaction scores.

What I recommended

  • A certification path with objective exams, so engineers could prove device-specific troubleshooting skills.
  • Digital job aids and interactive scenarios aimed at the troubleshooting gaps behind 35% of tickets.
  • Moving safety training to virtual instructor-led sessions to close protocol gaps during urgent repairs.

*Name and identifying details have been changed to protect the client's confidentiality.

SME Interview & Content Architecture

  • SME interviews
  • Scenario design
95%positive SME rating for accuracy and clarity

The challenge

SMEs know their material deeply, but they often hand over an information dump instead of the stories and decisions a scenario needs. For an emotional intelligence and difficult conversations module, I needed real examples I could turn into branching scenarios.

What I did

  • Wrote 21 targeted interview questions for "Week 5: Difficult Conversations," each aimed at a specific behavior.
  • Asked for the worst and best real examples, which gave me the raw material for branching scenarios.
  • Included questions about closing the conversation and how students react, so I could spot learning gaps before development started.
  • Used the answers to identify "fight or flight" reactions, which became feedback loops in the final module.

Result

  • Grounding the build in real scenarios up front meant fewer revision rounds later.
  • SMEs gave the content a 95% positive rating for accuracy and clarity.
  • Soft skills like reading non-verbal cues became lessons and assessments with clear, measurable objectives.
03

HR Behavioral Storyboard: Difficult Conversations

  • Wireframing
  • Branching scenarios
  • Storyline variables
50% fewerStoryline programming revisions

My internal storyboards are proprietary, so this sample shows how I turn SME interview notes into a build plan. Defining the variables (like v_RapportScore) and branches at the storyboard stage means fewer surprises once the course is in Storyline.

The challenge

In a difficult conversation, what happens next depends on what you say. I needed to turn the SMEs' qualitative insights into a branching storyboard a developer could program in Storyline.

What I did

  • Mapped each conversation as a decision tree with three paths: ideal (builds rapport and resolves the conflict), neutral (stalls, with cues toward better understanding) and failure (sets off a fight-or-flight reaction, with feedback on non-verbal cues).
  • Planned a "Rapport Score" variable to track the learner's choices across the module.
  • Scripted closing feedback based on how the learner handled each scenario.
  • Placed short Powtoon explainers and Canva infographics to break up long stretches of dialogue.

Result

  • SMEs walked through the conversation logic at the wireframe stage, which cut Storyline programming revisions by 50%.
  • The module answers likely questions before learners get confused.
  • Learners find out why a choice worked, not just whether they passed.

Quality Assurance

Medical software training has no room for errors, so every course I build goes through five review stages before it launches.

  1. SME validation in Review 360

    SMEs review and comment in Articulate Review 360 until software versions and clinical details are verified and signed off. Nothing moves into the build until they approve.

  2. Peer review

    Another designer reviews every module for instructional quality and consistency.

  3. Senior editor pass

    I do my own detailed edit, checking the interface, wording and Section 508 accessibility on every device.

  4. Learning lead review

    A learning lead confirms the course meets the business goals, brand standards and ISO 9001 requirements.

  5. Publishing and LMS testing

    I package the course as SCORM or xAPI and test it in the LMS, making sure bookmarking, completion and reporting all work before learners see it.

Every project also follows the same folder and naming system, from planning documents and storyboards to the published SCORM and xAPI packages, so any file can be traced during an audit.
